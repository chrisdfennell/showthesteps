Start here

Type the problem. The big box on the home page, and the search box in the bar at the top of every page, take any problem — 47 + 38, 3/4 ÷ 1/2, solve 3x + 2 = 11, area of a circle with radius 5, or a whole story problem — and open the helper that works it out.

Pilots: type an airport code like KSEA (or weather kden) to see its latest METAR decoded on the Flight Computer, or winds aloft SEA for the winds-aloft forecast. US airports only (National Weather Service data); the first time, it asks before anything is sent.

Or take a photo of it. The camera button beside the problem box reads a printed problem from a photo, a picture or a pasted screenshot, on your own device, and puts it in the box for you to check before it's worked out.

Or say it. The microphone button beside the camera turns what you say into the problem: say three fourths of twenty, x squared plus five x plus six equals zero or a whole story problem, and it's put in the box (3/4 of 20, x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0) and worked out. It works in Chrome, Edge and Safari.

Or pick a topic from Browse all topics (with buttons for each grade band), or from the menus in the bar at the top of every page: each section (Arithmetic, Fractions & money, Algebra & graphs, …) lists its helpers, with a link straight to each of their tabs.

Want the idea explained first? The lessons walk through how to do each kind of problem, with a worked example and links to try your own, and the Math at work lessons do the same for pilots, nurses, carpenters, electricians and cooks.

Each helper covers one topic and shows the work the way it's done on paper: one step at a time, with the reason for each step and a picture or diagram beside it, then the answer and a check that it makes sense.

On every helper

Five ways to work, in the buttons above the steps:

  • Show all work — every step at once.
  • One step at a time — try each step yourself, then reveal it (← →).
  • Check my answer — type your answer and get told if it's right. It knows 3/4, 0.75 and 75% are the same, and spots common mistakes ("you added the denominators").
  • Tutor me — before each step it asks what you'd do next, with a few choices and a hint after a wrong pick, then gives a score.
  • ▶ Play — watch the steps come up one at a time by themselves, each one read aloud, like a short video. Pause, go back or forward a step, start again, slower or faster, sound on or off (Space plays or pauses, ← → go a step). At the end it shows the answer. Pausing leaves you in One step at a time. A link with practice=play opens the problem and plays it.

Also on every page:

  • Copy link saves the exact problem: send it to a friend, a student or yourself. The QR code button (Q) opens it on a phone.
  • Embed (E) — the code to put the problem on your class website, Google Sites, Canvas or a blog: all the steps, one step at a time, or Check my answer, with a live preview. How embedding works.
  • Read aloud (R) reads the steps the way a teacher says them (“3 quarters”, “x squared”, “6 root 2”); Voice picks the voice and speed. Screen readers get the same words for the fractions, powers and answers.
  • Math keys (K) — a row of buttons for ÷ × − √ π ² and fractions. On phones they come up by themselves.
  • Make a problem for me makes a new practice problem of the kind you pick (N for the next one).
  • Light and dark (D), bigger or smaller text (+ −), and big screen for a projector (B).
  • Recent brings back problems you worked on, and each page opens where you left it.
  • Photo of the problem — the camera button reads a printed problem from a photo or screenshot. It works best on clear, printed text; handwriting often can't be read yet. The picture never leaves your device (the reading engine, about 7 MB, is downloaded once, the first time).
  • Say the problem — the microphone button beside the camera. Press it, say the problem, then pause (or press it again). Numbers become digits and math words become signs: forty seven plus thirty eight → 47 + 38, the square root of seventy two → sqrt(72), two and a half times six → 2 1/2 × 6, three dollars and fifty cents → $3.50, open parenthesis … close parenthesis for brackets. A story problem keeps its words. Check the box afterwards: a misheard word can be fixed by typing. Your browser (Chrome, Edge or Safari) turns the speech into text, usually with its maker's online speech service; a notice says so before the first use (Privacy). Esc stops without using what was heard. Firefox has no speech recognition, so there's no button there.
  • Common Core — under the heading, the standards the open tab practices; hover a code to read it, click it for the Standards page. The matching is ours, not official.
  • Add to assignment — collects the problem on the screen, exactly as it is, for an assignment (see below).
  • Report a problem — if an answer is wrong or a step is confusing, this opens a short form with the problem's link already filled in.
  • Press ? on any page for the full list of keys, and / to jump to the problem box.

Pages print cleanly, and once you've visited, the site works offline. On a phone you can add it to your home screen (in Safari: Share → Add to Home Screen; in Chrome: the menu → Install app).

Typing a problem

  • Ordinary wording is fine. "please solve 47+38", "what is forty-seven plus thirty-eight?", "simplify 18/24 step by step", "Problem 4: find the GCF of 12 and 18" — the helpers skip the extra words and read numbers written as words.
  • Signs: + − × ÷ or * / x between numbers. Powers with ^ (2^5, x^2), roots with sqrt or √, fractions as 3/4, mixed numbers as 2 3/4, percents with %.
  • One problem at a time. A numbered homework list ("1. Write an equation … 2. How many …") is fine in a story problem: the question numbers are skipped.
  • If a page can't read it, it says so instead of guessing, and usually says why or links to the helper that does that kind of problem. You can also fill in the boxes yourself, or pick the kind of problem.
  • Still stuck? Tell us what you typed — that's how the helpers learn new wording.

Story problems

Paste a whole word problem into The Story Problem. It works out what kind of story it is, shows what it understood under What the story says (fix anything it got wrong there), and works it out in the same steps every time: read it, say what's asked, list what you know, choose the operation (or write an equation), draw it, solve, and answer in a sentence with a check.

  • Grades K–5: adding to and taking from, putting together, comparing, equal groups, arrays and area, times as many, rates and unit prices, money and change, elapsed time, fractions of an amount, percents and discounts, two-step stories, and remainders that matter (vans needed, full boxes, leftovers).
  • Algebra: number puzzles, consecutive numbers, ages, perimeter and angles, two plans that cost the same, tickets and coins, distance-rate-time (opposite ways, toward each other, catching up, wind and current), working together, mixtures, interest, a fixed fee plus so much each, at most / at least, proportions, prices that change, groups tied together ("10 more adult tickets than children's"), and dozens more shapes of equation.
  • When the problem asks you to write an equation or expressions, the answer shows them too, along with the solution.
  • Some problems belong on another helper — probability, the normal curve, optimization, three equations — and the page says so and opens the right one with the problem filled in.

Tips: include the question sentence ("How many …?"); write numbers as digits when you can; if it picks the wrong kind, use Not right? Change it.

Making an assignment (no accounts)

  1. On any helper, set up a problem (type it, or press New problem) and press Add to assignment beside Copy link. Do that for every problem you want, on any helpers.
  2. Open the assignment builder (the "in your assignment" link beside the button). Put the problems in order, remove any, or preview one as students will see it.
  3. Give it a title, instructions and a due date. Choose whether students see the steps after answering, and whether they get more than one try.
  4. Press Share: make the link, then copy the link, show the QR code, or copy the ready-made text for Google Classroom or email.
  5. Students open the link, type their name and work the problems. At the end they copy a result line and send it to you.
  6. Paste everyone's lines into Class results: right or wrong on every problem, how many tries each took, and who handed in late.

The whole assignment is in the link, so links you've shared keep working; the draft you're building is kept only in this browser.

Worksheet versions

In the Worksheet Maker, pick Versions: 2, 3 or 4. Each version (A–D) has the same kinds of problems in the same order with different numbers, its letter in the header, and its own answer key (all keys at the end, or after each version). Shuffle the order in B–D also mixes the order within each section. Worksheet link gives a link and QR code that reopen the same worksheet, with the same numbers in every version.

Problems for a standard

On the Standards page, every standard has Make problems: Worksheet | Assignment. Set how many problems at the top (10 to start, up to 40 for each standard). Worksheet opens the Worksheet Maker with a section for each helper tab that practices the standard, the problems split across them, and the standard in the title; versions, the answer key and the worksheet link work as always. Assignment fills the assignment builder with that many new problems; if your assignment already has problems, it asks first whether to replace them, add the new ones after them, or keep it as it is. Tick Pick on several standards to put them all on one worksheet or one assignment (each gets its number of problems).

A standard with more tabs than problems takes one problem from each of that many tabs (the note at the top says which were left out). The links can be shared: practice/worksheets?std=6.RP.A.3&n=10, practice/sets?assignment&std=6.RP.A.3,6.RP.A.2&n=5.

Not yet: math we're still working on

The site doesn't do everything yet. These are known gaps — if one of them matters to you, say so; requests decide what comes next.

  • Counting with conditions — people who must sit together, codes with rules like "even numbers only", paths on a grid, identical things into boxes. The Counting Tree does plain counting, arrangements and combinations, and says when a condition is beyond it.
  • Handwriting in photos — the camera button reads printed problems; handwritten ones usually can't be read yet.
  • Saying a problem in Firefox — Firefox has no speech recognition built in, so the microphone button only shows in Chrome, Edge and Safari.
  • Optimizing with constraints — linear programming (the most profit from several limits). The Graph Paper shades the region, but doesn't yet find the best corner for you.
  • Calculus word problems — "what size box has the biggest volume" has to be set up as a function first; the Rate Machine then finds the maximum.
  • Binomial probability typed into The Bell Curve as a story — The Story Problem works it out; the Bell Curve needs the numbers in its boxes.
  • Geometry: Heron's formula for a triangle from three sides, and naming sides by their corners (AB, BC).
  • Algebra: literal equations (solve for r), compound inequalities, and systems in letters other than x and y on the Balance Scale.
  • Calculus: integration by parts and other advanced integrals.
  • Proofs, constructions with a compass and ruler, and anything beyond a first-year college course.
  • Other languages — the site reads and writes English only for now.

Each helper's own "Not yet" list (above, under The helpers) has the details for that topic.

Questions

Is it free? Yes — no accounts, no sign-up, no ads. See About.

Does it keep what I type? No. Every problem is worked out in your own browser; your settings and progress are saved only on your device. See Privacy.

Can I trust the answers? Every answer is worked with exact numbers and checked a second way, and the site runs a self-test of more than 22,000 checks before anything changes. If you find a wrong one, use Report a problem on that page.

Does it work on a phone or tablet? Yes, in any modern browser — and offline once you've visited.

Can I ask for a topic or a kind of problem? Please do: request it on GitHub.
