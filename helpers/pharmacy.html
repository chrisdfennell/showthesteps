    About the Pill Counter

    The Pill Counter works pharmacy technician math step by step: reading a sig, days’ supply, the quantity to dispense, alligation, percent and ratio strengths, dilutions, compounding a formula to a new amount, conversions and Roman numerals, pricing with AWP and dispensing fees, and storage temperatures. Each step gives the reason and the rounding rule, and an alligation problem is drawn on the alligation grid. It is for learning and certification-exam practice: at work, always follow the prescription and your pharmacy’s procedures, and have the pharmacist check your work.

    A worked example: mix 10% and 2% to make 500 g of 5%

    1. Set up the grid: the stronger 10% top left, the weaker 2% bottom left, and the 5% you want in the middle.
    2. Subtract along the diagonals: 5% − 2% = 3 parts of the 10%, and 10% − 5% = 5 parts of the 2%. That is 8 parts in all.
    3. Share the 500 g by the parts: 3/8 × 500 = 187.5 g of the 10%, and 5/8 × 500 = 312.5 g of the 2%.
    4. Check it: 10% of 187.5 g is 18.75 g and 2% of 312.5 g is 6.25 g, so the mix holds 25 g of drug, which is 5% of 500 g.

    Common questions

    How do I calculate days’ supply?

    Find how much is used in a day from the sig, then divide the quantity dispensed by it. 90 tablets at 1 tab TID is 3 tablets a day, so 90 ÷ 3 = 30 days. Some insurance plans and pharmacy systems have their own rules for days’ supply; follow yours.

    How many mL do I dispense for a liquid prescription?

    Work out the mL a day, then multiply by the days. For 1 tsp TID for 10 days: 1 tsp is 5 mL, three times a day is 15 mL a day, and 15 mL × 10 = 150 mL.

    What does a percent strength like 2% mean?

    For a solution (w/v), 2% means 2 g of drug in every 100 mL. So 250 mL of a 2% solution holds 250 × 2 ÷ 100 = 5 g. Creams and ointments are w/w (grams in 100 g), and a liquid in a liquid, like alcohol, is v/v.
