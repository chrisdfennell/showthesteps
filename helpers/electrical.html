    About the Circuit Board

    The Circuit Board works electrical math step by step: Ohm’s law, power in watts, series and parallel resistors, voltage drop in a wire, circuit loads and the 80% rule for a breaker, the cost of running an appliance, the resistor color code, capacitors and RC time constants, and AC and three-phase power. Each step says what to do and why, with the Ohm’s law triangle, the circuit and the resistor network drawn out. It is for learning and checking coursework: electrical work can kill, so follow your local electrical code and have a licensed electrician do or inspect real wiring.

    A worked example: voltage drop on 100 ft of 12 AWG copper at 16 A, 120 V

    1. Look up the wire: 12 AWG copper has 1.98 Ω per 1,000 ft (NEC Chapter 9, Table 8).
    2. Count the wire out and back: 200 ft, so 0.396 Ω. Forgetting the return wire halves the answer.
    3. Voltage drop = current × resistance: 16 × 0.396 = 6.336 V lost in the wire.
    4. As a percent: 6.336 ÷ 120 × 100 ≈ 5.28%, over the common 3% guideline, so the load gets 113.664 V.

    Common questions

    How do I use Ohm’s law to find the current?

    Divide the voltage by the resistance: I = V ÷ R. With 12 V across 4 Ω, the current is 12 ÷ 4 = 3 A, and the power is 12 V × 3 A = 36 W.

    How much load can I put on a 20 amp circuit?

    A common rule of practice keeps a continuous load (3 hours or more) to 80% of the breaker: 16 A, or 1,920 W at 120 V. A 1,500 W heater and an 800 W microwave are 2,300 W, and 2,300 ÷ 120 ≈ 19.17 A, over the 80% limit. The rule is not your local code: follow the code where you live.

    How do I work out what an appliance costs to run?

    Change the watts to kilowatts, multiply by the hours to get kWh, then multiply by the price. A 1,500 W heater 8 hours a day is 1.5 × 8 = 12 kWh, and at $0.15 per kWh that is $1.80 a day, $54.00 a month or $657.00 a year.
