How to Find the Wind Correction Angle and Ground Speed

A crosswind pushes an airplane sideways, so to fly a straight course over the ground the pilot points the nose a little into the wind. That small turn is the wind correction angle (WCA). The wind also changes the ground speed.

For learning only. This lesson is for studying and ground-school work, not for planning a real flight. Use official weather, charts and flight-planning tools, and the numbers in your aircraft’s handbook (POH).

Try it with your own problem ↓

The steps

  1. Write down the true course (TC), the true airspeed (TAS) and the wind: the direction it blows from and its speed. Winds-aloft forecasts give true directions.
  2. Find the wind angle: wind direction − course. Plus means the wind is from the right, minus from the left.
  3. Find the crosswind part: wind speed × sin(wind angle). Then sin WCA = crosswind ÷ TAS, so WCA = sin⁻¹(crosswind ÷ TAS).
  4. Turn into the wind: true heading = course + WCA for a wind from the right, course − WCA for a wind from the left.
  5. Ground speed = TAS × cos(WCA) − wind × cos(wind angle). A headwind makes it less than the TAS, a tailwind more.

A worked example

You want a true course of 090° at a true airspeed of 120 knots. The wind is from 040° at 20 knots. Find the WCA, the true heading and the ground speed.

  1. Wind angle: 040° − 090° = −50°. The wind is 50° to the left of the course, from ahead.
  2. Crosswind part: 20 × sin 50° = 20 × 0.766 = 15.32 kt.
  3. sin WCA = 15.32 ÷ 120 = 0.1277, so WCA = sin⁻¹ 0.1277 ≈ 7.34°, about 7°.
  4. The wind is from the left, so turn left: true heading = 090° − 7° = 083°.
  5. Ground speed: 120 × cos 7.34° − 20 × cos 50° = 119.02 − 12.86 = 106.16, about 106 kt.

Fly a true heading of 083° (a WCA of 7° left), and expect a ground speed of about 106 knots.

Why it works

Think of the airplane’s motion as two arrows added together: its heading at the true airspeed, and the wind’s drift. For the sum to point exactly along the course, the sideways parts must cancel, so the airplane’s sideways part, TAS × sin WCA, has to match the wind’s crosswind part. That is where sin WCA = crosswind ÷ TAS comes from. The parts along the course then add up to the ground speed.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the wind as where it blows to. Winds are reported by where they come from. A wind from 040° blows toward 220°.
  • Turning the wrong way. Turn the nose into the wind: a wind from the left means a heading less than the course.
  • Mixing true and magnetic. Winds aloft are true, so work with the true course, then apply the variation to get the magnetic heading.
  • Using the TAS as the ground speed. With a headwind part you cover less ground than the airspeed says, so the time and fuel go up.

Questions people ask

Is there a quick rule of thumb for the WCA?

The most the wind can push you is about 60 × wind ÷ TAS degrees: 60 × 20 ÷ 120 = 10° here. A wind 50° off the course gives about 50/60 of that, roughly 8°, close to the 7° worked out.

How do I get the magnetic heading?

Apply the variation from the chart: east is least (subtract), west is best (add). With 10° east variation, a true heading of 083° is a magnetic heading of 073°.

Why is the ground speed less than the airspeed here?

The wind comes from ahead of the wing, so part of it, 20 × cos 50° ≈ 12.9 kt, is a headwind.
